Weighted Quantile Loss, explained

November 14, 2026 · 4 min · Mina Habib
Table of Contents

  • One formula
  • A tiny worked example
  • How it compares to the usual metrics
  • When to use it

Most forecast accuracy metrics answer one question: how far off was the prediction? Weighted quantile loss answers a different one. It asks whether your forecast got the uncertainty right, and it cares more about the items that matter.

That makes it a good fit for demand forecasting, where you rarely want a single number. You want a range, and you want to be more careful about the products that move a lot of volume.

The intuition#

Start with a single prediction. Say you forecast demand for next week and you want the 90th percentile, the level you’ll only exceed 10% of the time. Useful for setting stock so you rarely run out.

If the real demand comes in above your forecast, that’s bad. You under-stocked. If it comes in below, that’s also not free, but it’s a different kind of wrong. Quantile loss lets you penalize those two mistakes by different amounts, depending on which percentile you asked for.

For the 90th percentile, being too low is penalized nine times harder than being too high. That asymmetry is the whole point: it pushes the forecast up until, over many weeks, it lands above the truth about 90% of the time. Exactly what a 90th percentile should do.

One formula#

For a single actual value $y$, a forecast $q$ at quantile level $\tau$ (a number between 0 and 1), the quantile loss is:

$$ \mathrm{QL}_\tau(y, q) = \max\big(\tau,(y - q),; (\tau - 1),(y - q)\big) $$

Read it as two cases:

  • If you under-forecast ($y > q$): the loss is $\tau,(y - q)$.
  • If you over-forecast ($y < q$): the loss is $(1 - \tau),(q - y)$.

At $\tau = 0.9$, under-forecasting costs 0.9 per unit and over-forecasting costs 0.1 per unit. Nine to one.

Weighted quantile loss then sums this over all items and all time, and divides by total actual volume so big and small series can sit in the same number:

$$ \mathrm{WQL}(\tau) = \frac{\sum_i \mathrm{QL}_\tau(y_i, q_i)}{\sum_i |y_i|} $$

The division is what makes it “weighted”: items with more volume contribute more to both the numerator and the denominator, so the metric naturally pays more attention to them. A high-volume product being off matters more than a tiny one being off by the same percentage.

A tiny worked example#

One item, one week. You forecast the 90th percentile as $q = 100$.

  • Actual comes in at $y = 130$ (you under-forecast by 30). Loss $= 0.9 \times 30 = 27$.
  • Different week, actual comes in at $y = 80$ (you over-forecast by 20). Loss $= 0.1 \times 20 = 2$.

Same size miss in units, very different penalty. The metric is telling you that for a 90th percentile, running short is the expensive mistake.

How it compares to the usual metrics#

  • MAE (mean absolute error) averages the size of the miss. Simple and robust, but it only scores a single point forecast, and it treats over and under the same. No notion of a quantile.
  • RMSE (root mean squared error) squares the errors first, so a few big misses dominate. Good when large errors are especially costly, but sensitive to outliers and, again, point-only and symmetric.
  • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) divides each error by the actual. Easy to explain as a percentage, but it blows up when actuals are near zero, and it quietly over-weights low-volume items. In intermittent demand it can be useless or undefined.
  • WAPE (weighted absolute percentage error) fixes MAPE’s main flaw by dividing total error by total actual volume, so it’s volume-weighted and stable near zero. It’s basically WQL’s median sibling: WAPE lines up with WQL at $\tau = 0.5$ up to a scale factor. But it still scores one point forecast, not a range.
  • Quantile loss (unweighted) has the asymmetry WQL has, but without the volume normalization, so a handful of large series can swamp it and you can’t compare across datasets of different scale.

The short version. MAE, RMSE, MAPE and WAPE all grade a single number. WQL grades a distribution. It checks whether your forecast’s percentiles are honest, and it does that in a volume-aware way.

When to use it#

Reach for WQL when all three are true:

  1. You produce probabilistic forecasts (percentiles or a full distribution), not just one number.
  2. The cost of being wrong is asymmetric. Running out hurts more than overstocking, or the reverse.
  3. Volume varies a lot across items and you want the metric to care proportionally.

If you only have a point forecast, WQL has nothing extra to offer, so use WAPE or MAE. And it’s worth tracking more than one quantile (say the 50th and the 90th): the 50th tells you if the forecast is centered, the 90th tells you if the tail is calibrated.
